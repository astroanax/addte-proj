\subsection{Introduction and Inspiration}

We apply Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) to analyze financial time-series data. Our approach is inspired by the work of Mann and Kutz \cite{kutz2015dmd}, who demonstrated the application of DMD-based algorithmic trading strategies on portfolios of financial data.

Mann and Kutz showed that DMD, originally developed for fluid dynamics applications, is a powerful equation-free, data-driven method capable of decomposing complex dynamical systems into low-rank modes with known temporal behavior. In the financial context, DMD decomposes stock portfolio data into ``portfolio modes'' --- linear combinations of stocks that exhibit coherent temporal dynamics. The method approximates the system as $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_k$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is the Koopman operator, and the solution takes the form:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} b_k \boldsymbol{\psi}_k e^{\omega_k t}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\psi}_k$ are the DMD modes (spatial/portfolio structures), $\omega_k$ are the eigenvalues (temporal dynamics), and $b_k$ are the mode amplitudes. The real part of $\omega_k$ indicates growth ($\text{Re}(\omega_k) > 0$) or decay ($\text{Re}(\omega_k) < 0$), while the imaginary part represents oscillation frequency.

The key insight from Kutz's work is that DMD can identify transient, evanescent signals in financial data by extracting dominant modes and their time evolution. Modes with positive growth rates represent bullish trends, while decaying modes indicate bearish behavior. This decomposition enables short-term prediction and trading strategy formulation based on the dominant market dynamics.

\subsection{Methodology}

\subsubsection{DMD Algorithm}\footnote{We did not use the given DMD.m code, but implemented our own DMD instead}

The DMD algorithm was implemented following the standard procedure:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data normalization:} Zero-mean, unit-variance normalization to ensure fair comparison across stocks with different price scales.
    
    \item \textbf{Time-shifted matrices:} Construct $\mathbf{X}_1 = [\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{M-1}]$ and $\mathbf{X}_2 = [\mathbf{x}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_M]$
    
    \item \textbf{SVD decomposition:} $\mathbf{X}_1 = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^*$, truncated to rank $r$
    
    \item \textbf{Reduced DMD matrix:} $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{U}_r^* \mathbf{X}_2 \mathbf{V}_r \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1}$
    
    \item \textbf{Eigendecomposition:} $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$
    
    \item \textbf{DMD modes:} $\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \mathbf{X}_2 \mathbf{V}_r \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r^{-1} \mathbf{W}$
    
    \item \textbf{Continuous eigenvalues:} $\omega_k = \ln(\lambda_k)/\Delta t$
\end{enumerate}

The rank $r$ was chosen to capture 90\% of the total energy in the singular value spectrum.

\subsection{Methodology}

\subsubsection{Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)}

The DFT converts a discrete time-domain signal $x[n]$ of length $N$ into its frequency-domain representation:
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot e^{-j2\pi kn/N}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1
\end{equation}

The single-sided amplitude spectrum is computed as:
\begin{equation}
    |X[k]|_{\text{single}} = \frac{2|X[k]|}{N}, \quad k = 1, \ldots, N/2
\end{equation}

The frequency resolution is $\Delta f = f_s / N$, where $f_s$ is the sampling frequency.

\subsubsection{Filter Design}

We implemented three types of ideal filters with Gaussian roll-off (to minimize ringing artifacts) in the frequency domain:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\begin{tabular}{@{}lccp{6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Filter Type} & \textbf{Cutoff} & \textbf{Passband} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\midrule
Low-pass & 50 Hz & $0 - 50$ Hz & Removes high-frequency noise \\

Band-pass & 50--200 Hz & $50 - 200$ Hz & Isolates gravitational wave chirp \\

The filters were implemented in the frequency domain by multiplying the signal's FFT by the filter transfer function $H(f)$:
\begin{equation}
    Y(f) = X(f) \cdot H(f)
\end{equation}
and then applying the inverse FFT to obtain the filtered time-domain signal.

\subsubsection{Effect of Low-Pass Filter (50 Hz cutoff)}
The low-pass filter removes high-frequency components above 50 Hz. In the time domain, this results in a smoother signal with reduced high-frequency noise. However, since the gravitational wave chirp extends to higher frequencies ($\sim$250 Hz at merger), significant signal content is also removed.

\subsubsection{Effect of High-Pass Filter (100 Hz cutoff)}
The high-pass filter removes low-frequency components below 100 Hz, effectively eliminating baseline drift and low-frequency noise. This is particularly useful for LIGO data, which suffers from seismic noise at low frequencies. The filtered signal retains the higher-frequency portion of the chirp.

\subsubsection{Effect of Band-Pass Filter (50--200 Hz)}
The band-pass filter isolates the frequency range most relevant to the gravitational wave chirp. This is the optimal filter choice for gravitational wave detection, as it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Removes low-frequency seismic noise
    \item Removes high-frequency instrumental noise
    \item Preserves the chirp signal in its characteristic frequency band
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

The analysis successfully revealed the frequency content of the LIGO gravitational wave signal.
The Low-pass filtering smooths the signal but removes high-frequency signal content, high-pass filtering removes baseline drift and low-frequency noise, and band-pass filtering is optimal for isolating the gravitational wave chirp
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Introduction}

This problem demonstrates fundamental discrete-time signal operations including time shifting and digital filtering. We implement both FIR (Finite Impulse Response) and IIR (Infinite Impulse Response) filters and compare their effects using DFT analysis.

\subsection{Signal Selection: Mario Coin Sound}

For an engaging and interpretable demonstration, we chose to model the iconic Mario ``coin'' sound - the rising arpeggio ``bling!'' heard when collecting a coin in Nintendo's Super Mario games. This signal is a universally known sound effect, and contains both smooth transitions and sharp changes

The discrete signal is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    x[n] = [0, 2, 5, 8, 10, 12, 10, 8, 5, 3, 1, 0]
\end{equation}
with $N = 12$ samples and 10 non-zero elements.

\subsection{Signal Operations}

\subsubsection{(i) Original Signal $x[n]$}

The original Mario coin sound pattern represents the characteristic rising-falling envelope of the ``bling!'' sound.

\subsubsection{(ii) Time Delay: $x[n-2]$}

The signal $x[n-2]$ represents the original signal delayed by 2 samples (shifted right). In discrete-time systems, this delay operation is fundamental for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Buffer/memory operations
    \item Echo effects in audio processing
    \item Implementing FIR filters
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{(iii) Time Advance: $x[n+1]$}

The signal $x[n+1]$ represents the original signal advanced by 1 sample (shifted left). Note that this is a non-causal operation --- it requires knowledge of future values.

\subsubsection{(iv) FIR Filter}

The FIR filter is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = 0.2 \cdot x[n] + 0.3 \cdot x[n-1] + 0.2 \cdot x[n-2]
\end{equation}

This is a \textbf{3-tap weighted moving average filter} with coefficients $[0.2, 0.3, 0.2]$. Properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type:} FIR (no feedback)
    \item \textbf{Effect:} Low-pass filtering (smoothing)
    \item \textbf{Stability:} Always stable (no poles)
    \item \textbf{Phase:} Linear phase (symmetric coefficients)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{(v) IIR Filter}

The IIR filter is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    y[n] = 0.2 \cdot x[n] + 0.3 \cdot x[n-1] + 0.5 \cdot y[n-1], \quad y[-1] = 0
\end{equation}

This is a \textbf{recursive filter with feedback}. Properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type:} IIR (has feedback from $y[n-1]$)
    \item \textbf{Effect:} Stronger low-pass filtering + ``ringing''
    \item \textbf{Stability:} Stable if $|0.5| < 1$ (pole inside unit circle)
    \item \textbf{Memory:} Infinite impulse response due to feedback
\end{itemize}

\section{Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy)}
%==============================================================================

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{doublepen.jpg}
    \caption{Double pendulum}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Introduction}

This problem applies the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) algorithm to discover governing equations from data. SINDy, developed by Brunton, Proctor, and Kutz \cite{sindy2016}, is a powerful data-driven method that identifies parsimonious (sparse) dynamical models from time-series measurements.

The key insight of SINDy is that most physical systems are governed by equations with only a few active terms, even when expressed in a large library of candidate functions. By leveraging sparsity-promoting regression, SINDy can automatically discover these governing equations without prior knowledge of the system.

\subsection{System Selection: Double Pendulum}

We chose the double pendulum as our test system because it is a classic mechanics problem and a fundamental example in non dynamics and chaos. The coupled oscillators demonstrate how SINDy can identify coupled differential equations. It exhibits chaotic behavior, but its linear version shows harmonic oscillation.

\subsubsection{Double Pendulum Equations (Small-Angle Approximation)}

For a double pendulum with equal masses $m$ and equal lengths $L$, the state variables are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta_1, \theta_2$: angular positions of the two pendulums
    \item $\omega_1 = \dot{\theta}_1, \omega_2 = \dot{\theta}_2$: angular velocities
\end{itemize}

Using the small-angle approximation ($\sin\theta \approx \theta$, $\cos\theta \approx 1$), the linearized equations of motion are:
\begin{align}
    \frac{d\theta_1}{dt} &= \omega_1 \\
    \frac{d\theta_2}{dt} &= \omega_2 \\
    \frac{d\omega_1}{dt} &= -2\frac{g}{L}\theta_1 + \frac{g}{L}\theta_2 \\
    \frac{d\omega_2}{dt} &= 2\frac{g}{L}\theta_1 - 2\frac{g}{L}\theta_2
\end{align}

With $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$ and $L = 1$ m, we have $g/L = 9.81$ rad/s$^2$.

\subsection{Methodology: SINDy Algorithm}

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation}

Given time-series data $\mathbf{x}(t)$ and its derivative $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)$, SINDy seeks a sparse representation:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}} = \boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathbf{x}) \boldsymbol{\Xi}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\mathbf{x})$ is a library of candidate functions and $\boldsymbol{\Xi}$ contains the sparse coefficients.

\subsubsection{Polynomial Library}

For $n = 4$ state variables ($\theta_1, \theta_2, \omega_1, \omega_2$) and polynomial order 2, the library contains:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Theta} = [1, \theta_1, \theta_2, \omega_1, \omega_2, \theta_1^2, \theta_1\theta_2, \theta_1\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_2^2]
\end{equation}
yielding 15 candidate functions per equation.

\subsubsection{Sparse Regression}

The coefficients $\boldsymbol{\Xi}$ are found using Sequential Thresholded Least Squares (STLS):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Solve $\boldsymbol{\Xi} = \boldsymbol{\Theta}^\dagger \dot{\mathbf{x}}$ (least squares)
    \item Set coefficients below threshold $\lambda$ to zero
    \item Repeat until convergence
\end{enumerate}

We used $\lambda = 0.1$ for sparsification.

\subsubsection{Implementation}

The analysis was performed using the SINDy MATLAB code provided by Brunton et al., modified for the double pendulum system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training data:} 10,001 points from $t \in [0, 10]$ with $dt = 0.001$
    \item \textbf{Initial condition:} $[\theta_1, \theta_2, \omega_1, \omega_2]^T = [0.2, 0.1, 0, 0]^T$ rad
    \item \textbf{Noise level:} 0.1\% Gaussian noise added to derivatives
    \item \textbf{Integration:} \texttt{ode45} with tolerances $10^{-12}$
\end{itemize}

\section{Cross-Correlation for Audio Pattern Matching}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Introduction}

This problem demonstrates the application of \textbf{cross-correlation} for audio pattern matching --- the fundamental technique behind music recognition services like Shazam. Cross-correlation measures the similarity between two signals as a function of the time-lag applied to one of them, enabling detection of where a pattern occurs within a longer signal.

\subsection{Mathematical Background}

\subsubsection{Cross-Correlation Definition}

For discrete signals $x[n]$ and $y[n]$, the cross-correlation is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    (x \star y)[k] = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \cdot y[n+k]
\end{equation}

The lag $k$ at which the cross-correlation achieves its maximum indicates the time offset where the two signals are most similar.

\subsubsection{Relationship to Convolution}

Cross-correlation is closely related to convolution:
\begin{equation}
    x \star y = x * \text{flip}(y)
\end{equation}
where $\text{flip}(y)[n] = y[-n]$ is the time-reversed signal. This relationship allows efficient computation via FFT.

\subsubsection{Normalized Cross-Correlation}

To obtain a correlation coefficient independent of signal amplitude:
\begin{equation}
    r_{xy}[k] = \frac{(x \star y)[k]}{\|x\| \cdot \|y\|}
\end{equation}
where $\|x\| = \sqrt{\sum_n |x[n]|^2}$ is the signal energy.

\subsection{Introduction}

This problem demonstrates the application of SVD for image compression. SVD provides an optimal low-rank approximation of matrices, enabling significant data compression while preserving the essential features of images.

\subsection{Mathematical Background}

\subsubsection{SVD Decomposition}

Any matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ can be decomposed as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^T
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$: Left singular vectors (orthonormal)
    \item $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$: Diagonal matrix of singular values $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$
    \item $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$: Right singular vectors (orthonormal)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Low-Rank Approximation}

The rank-$k$ approximation retains only the $k$ largest singular values:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_k = \mathbf{U}_k \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k \mathbf{V}_k^T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Eckart-Young Theorem}

The rank-$k$ SVD approximation is \textit{optimal} in the Frobenius norm:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_k = \arg\min_{\text{rank}(\mathbf{B}) = k} \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\|_F
\end{equation}

The approximation error is:
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A}_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=k+1}^{r} \sigma_i^2}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Compression Ratio}

The original image requires $m \times n$ values. The rank-$k$ approximation requires:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Storage}_k = k \cdot (m + n + 1)
\end{equation}
for storing $\mathbf{U}_k$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$, and $\mathbf{V}_k$. The compression ratio is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Compression Ratio} = \frac{m \times n}{k \cdot (m + n + 1)}
\end{equation}

This problem demonstrates least squares polynomial regression for curve fitting and the bias-variance trade-off in model selection. We apply this to real-world financial data: the INR/USD exchange rate over the past year.

\subsection{Mathematical Background}

\subsubsection{Least Squares Problem}

Given data points $(t_i, y_i)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, we seek polynomial coefficients $\mathbf{c} = [c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_d]^T$ that minimize:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\mathbf{c}} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{A}$ is the Vandermonde matrix:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & t_1 & t_1^2 & \cdots & t_1^d \\
        1 & t_2 & t_2^2 & \cdots & t_2^d \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        1 & t_n & t_n^2 & \cdots & t_n^d
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Normal Equations}

The solution satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{y} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{c} = (\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{y}
\end{equation}

In MATLAB, this is computed as $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A} \backslash \mathbf{y}$ using QR decomposition for numerical stability.

\subsubsection{Model Selection Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$R^2$ (Coefficient of Determination):}
    \begin{equation}
        R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Adjusted $R^2$:} Penalizes model complexity
    \begin{equation}
        R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2) \frac{n-1}{n-d-1}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{MSE (Mean Squared Error):}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

Degree 1 (linear fit) clearly underfits the data:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low $R^2$ (0.54): Only explains 54\% of variance
    \item Systematic residual patterns: Curved structure visible in residuals
    \item High bias: Model too simple to capture the trend
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Good Fit (Medium Degree)}

Degrees 2--5 provide reasonable fits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $R^2$ between 0.68--0.88
    \item More random residual distribution
    \item Balanced bias-variance trade-off
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Overfitting (High Degree)}

Degree 20 shows signs of overfitting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Train MSE (0.096) $<$ Test MSE (0.123)
    \item High variance: Model fits noise in training data
    \item Oscillations between data points
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Economic Interpretation}

The exchange rate trend reflects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Overall depreciation:} INR weakened from $\sim$84 to $\sim$90 per USD
    \item \textbf{Volatility periods:} Sharp movements correspond to economic events
    \item \textbf{Polynomial capture:} Higher degrees needed to model complex dynamics
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Model Selection}

Based on cross-validation, degree 10 provides the best balance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lowest test MSE (0.1285)
    \item No significant train-test gap
    \item High $R^2$ (0.9257) without overfitting
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

Least squares polynomial regression on INR/USD exchange rate demonstrated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Underfitting:} Low-degree polynomials fail to capture trends
    \item \textbf{Good fit:} Medium degrees balance bias and variance
    \item \textbf{Overfitting:} High degrees fit noise, poor generalization
    \item \textbf{Cross-validation:} Essential for model selection
\end{enumerate}

The analysis shows the INR depreciated against USD over the past year, with degree 10 polynomial providing the best predictive model.
